# The Blame Transfer Protocol

There is a rule embedded so deep in organisational DNA that most leaders do not even recognise it as a rule. It sounds like responsibility. It feels like justice. It is neither.

The rule: *When something goes wrong, find who is responsible.*

Not what went wrong. Not why the system allowed it. **Who.**

## How the Grip Tightens

The protocol runs automatically. A project fails. A deadline slips. A client complains. The first question in the room is not "what happened?" but "who was leading this?"

The answer is always the same: someone visible enough to absorb blame, but not powerful enough to deflect it. A mid-level manager. A team lead. The person who sent the last status update.

This is not accountability. This is **blame gravity**: the natural tendency of failure to fall downward through the hierarchy until it lands on someone who cannot push it further.

## The Release

The release requires separating two things the culture fused together: **accountability** and **punishment**.

Accountability says: "I own this outcome. Here is what I will change."
Punishment says: "You caused this outcome. Here is what we will do to you."

The first creates learning. The second creates hiding. An organisation that punishes failure will still have failures. It will just stop hearing about them until they become catastrophic.

## The Residue

The grip leaves marks on the people who were scapegoated. They become cautious. Risk-averse. They learn to document everything, not for clarity but for defence. They learn to never be the most visible person on a failing project.

The organisation loses them twice: once when it blames them, and again when their caution removes them from any work that matters.

> *"Deliberate before you decide. To deliberate after deciding is disgrace."* (Thirukkural 846)

Scapegoating is the ultimate post-decision disgrace: blaming after the fact instead of deliberating before.
