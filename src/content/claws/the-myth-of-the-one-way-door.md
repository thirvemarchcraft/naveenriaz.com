# The Myth of the One-Way Door

Every organisation has a rule that sounds like wisdom: *some decisions cannot be undone, so treat them with extreme care.*

This is true. Some decisions are genuinely irreversible. An acquisition. A public statement. A key hire fired.

The trap is not in the rule. The trap is in its application. When every decision is treated as irreversible, the organisation does not become careful. It becomes frozen.

## How the Grip Tightens

It starts with one genuine one-way door. A decision that truly could not be reversed. The organisation learned from it: "We should have been more careful." A reasonable lesson.

But the lesson metastasises. Soon, every decision is treated with the same gravity. Tool selection? One-way door. Sprint priority? One-way door. Meeting cadence? Somehow, also a one-way door.

The committee grows. The analysis deepens. The slide decks multiply. Nobody says "this is a reversible decision and we should just try it." Because nobody wants to be the person who underestimated the stakes.

## The Release

The release requires a simple classification that most organisations resist:

**Type 1 (One-Way Door):** Genuinely irreversible. Warrants deep analysis. Examples: M&A, structural reorg, market exit.

**Type 2 (Two-Way Door):** Reversible. Should be made quickly by small teams. Examples: feature flags, tool selection, process changes, sprint priorities.

The ratio is roughly 95:5. Ninety-five percent of organisational decisions are Type 2. But most organisations treat 95% as Type 1.

## The Residue

Even after the classification is learned, the reflex persists. Before every decision, there is a moment of hesitation: "but what if we cannot undo this?" The healthy version of that question leads to honest classification. The trapped version leads to another meeting.

> *"Those who foresee what is coming are never shaken by what arrives."* (Thirukkural 467)

Foresight is not about predicting every outcome. It is about knowing which doors swing both ways.
